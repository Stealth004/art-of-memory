The Technique, in One Paragraph

Artificial memory, the ancients taught, is built from exactly two things:

"The artificial memory is established from places and images" Constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus — Rhetorica ad Herennium 3.16.29, the stock definition "to be forever repeated down the ages" (Yates)

Places are an ordered series of stations — rooms of a house, corners, arches — memorized until you can traverse them forwards or backwards from any point. Images are striking, active pictures standing for the things you must remember, deposited in the places in order. To recall, you walk the route in your mind and collect the deposits.

Frances Yates on the name of the thing itself:

"The word 'mnemotechnics' hardly conveys what the artificial memory of Cicero may have been like" Frances A. Yates, The Art of Memory (1966), p. 4

Frances Yates's The Art of Memory (1966)

Her thesis

Yates — of the Warburg Institute, after sixteen years of study that began when Ernst Gombrich handed her Camillo's little treatise on the memory theatre — argues that the art of memory is not a marginal bag of mnemonic tricks but a major, continuous thread in Western intellectual history:

  • Classical: the system of places and images, invented (in legend) by Simonides, fully stated in the Rhetorica ad Herennium.
  • Medieval: detached from oratory and re-anchored in ethics — Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas treat memory as part of the cardinal virtue of Prudence.
  • Renaissance: fused with Lullism and Hermetic-Cabalist thought in the memory systems of Giulio Camillo, Giordano Bruno, and Robert Fludd.
  • Modern: in her boldest claim, the tradition fed the early-modern quest for a universal method of knowledge and the growth of scientific method. Yates disputed

Where scholars push back Yates disputed

1. Too narrow a foundation — Mary Carruthers

Carruthers argues Yates gives the impression "that learned mnemonics are a development only of 'the art of memory' described in the Rhetorica ad Herennium" and "that interest in mnemonics is to be identified with Neoplatonism." Her The Book of Memory (1990) recast medieval memory as a broader monastic, meditative, and compositional craft — and has become the standard scholarly authority.

2. Overstated Hermeticism

Critics hold that Yates overstates the mystical side of Renaissance mnemonics. On Bruno's wheels specifically, Rita Sturlese and others argue the signs "are not really 'icons'... therefore, they are not magical, or amulets: they are arbitrary signs" — a linguistic generator and manual of mental experimentation rather than magic.

3. The science claim is speculative

That the memory arts fed the growth of scientific method via Lullist combinatorics and Leibniz's universal characteristic is widely regarded as suggestive but speculative rather than demonstrated.

4. Greek origins are older than the Latin sources

The Dissoi logoi fragment (c. 400 BC) documents a Greek memory art older than the Latin sources — a qualification of Yates's emphasis on the three Latin sources, not a refutation.

The Ancient Records

Simonides of Ceos — the founding legend

The Greek poet Simonides (c. 556–468 BC) is the art's legendary inventor. The story, told by Cicero (De Oratore 2.86) and Quintilian (Institutio Oratoria XI.2), runs: at a banquet in Thessaly, Simonides is called outside just before the roof collapses, crushing the guests beyond recognition. He identifies each body by remembering where every guest had reclined at table — and infers that orderly arrangement is the best aid to memory.

Cicero's moral of the story:

"He inferred that persons desiring to train this faculty must select localities and form mental images of the facts they wish to remember and store those images in the localities, with the result that the arrangement of the localities will preserve the order of the facts, and the images of the facts will designate the facts themselves, and we shall employ the localities and images respectively as a wax writing tablet and the letters written on it." Cicero, De Oratore 2.86 (Sutton & Rackham translation)

Treat the tale as legend, not history — a point the ancient sources themselves invite. Quintilian notes "great disagreement among our authorities" over who the ode honored and which town it happened in, and dismisses the Castor-and-Pollux rescue as "purely fictitious."

Rhetorica ad Herennium (c. 86–82 BC) — the fullest ancient treatment

The anonymous Book 3 (3.16–24) gives the complete rules. The Latin line, verified:

Constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus Ad Herennium 3.16.29

Rules for places

Places must be small, complete, conspicuous — "such as a house, an intercolumnar space, a corner, an arch, or the like" — and above all form an ordered series, traversable from any point, forwards or backwards:

"We should therefore, if we desire to memorize a large number of items, equip ourselves with a large number of loci, so that in these we may set a large number of images. I likewise think it obligatory to have these loci in a series, so that we may never by confusion in their order be prevented from following the images — proceeding from any loci we wish, whatsoever its place in the series, and whether we go forwards or backwards." Ad Herennium 3.16.29 (Caplan translation)

Rules for images — imagines agentes

Images must be striking and doing something:

"We ought, then, to set up images of a kind that can adhere longest in the memory. And we shall do so if we establish likenesses as striking as possible; if we set up images that are not many or vague, but doing something; if we assign to them exceptional beauty or singular ugliness; if we dress some of them with crowns or purple cloaks, for example, so that the likeness may be more distinct to us." Ad Herennium 3.22.37 (Caplan translation)

The fuller passage adds: images may be "stained with blood or soiled with mud or smeared with red paint, so that its form is more striking," and given "certain comic effects... for that, too, will ensure our remembering them more readily."

Refresh by repetition

"again and again... run over rapidly in the mind all the original places in order to refresh the images" Ad Herennium, on rehearsal

Quintilian — the practical skeptic

"Some regard memory as being no more than one of nature's gifts; and this view is no doubt true to a great extent; but, like everything else, memory may be improved by cultivation." Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria XI.2 (Butler translation)
  • Why it works: "it is an assistance to the memory if localities are sharply impressed upon the mind, a view the truth of which everyone may realise by practical experiment" — return to a place and you remember what happened there.
  • The method: choose a spacious, varied place "such as a spacious house divided into a number of rooms"; invent symbols — "an anchor" for navigation, "some weapon" for warfare; place the first thought in the forecourt, the second in the living-room, and so on; then "all these places are visited in turn and the various deposits are demanded from their custodians." Places may be "real or imaginary."
  • His caution: the device works for reproducing names in order, but "will... be of less service in learning the various parts of a set speech. For thoughts do not call up the same images as material things." And his rule of thumb:
"If a speech of some length has to be committed to memory, it will be well to learn it piecemeal, since there is nothing so bad for the memory as being overburdened." Quintilian, XI.2.27

Aristotle — the laws of association

De Memoria et Reminiscentia (c. 350 BC): recollection works because "one movement has by nature another that succeeds it in regular order" — we hunt from a present thought "from something either similar, or contrary, to what we seek, or else from that which is contiguous with it." Similarity, contrast, contiguity — two millennia before the associationists. Aristotle also notes that a single striking experience can outlast many repetitions, and that "sometimes a place brings memories back to us."

Aquinas — memory as a virtue

The medieval pivot: because "Tullius" (Cicero) says memory is part of Prudence, and "Tullius" also teaches an artificial memory, the medievals concluded that artificial memory is part of the virtue of Prudence (Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 49, a. 1):

"Now experience is the result of many memories... and therefore prudence requires the memory of many things. Hence memory is fittingly accounted a part of prudence." Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 49, a. 1

"Memory not only arises from nature, but is also aided by art and diligence." His four rules:

  1. Unwonted images — "some suitable yet somewhat unwonted illustration," for "the unwonted strikes us more"; simple impressions "easily slip from the mind, unless they be tied as it were to some corporeal image."
  2. Order — "carefully consider and set in order, so that he may pass easily from one memory to another."
  3. Earnest attention — "be anxious and earnest about the things we wish to remember"; "anxiety preserves the figures of images entire."
  4. Repeated reflection — "we should often reflect on the things we wish to remember," for "reflection preserves memories... 'custom is a second nature.'"

The method of loci, as actually practiced

  1. Memorize a sequence of places — a house, street, or imagined building — until it runs forwards and backwards without hesitation.
  2. Convert each item into a striking, active image — beautiful or ugly, crowned or bloodied, doing something.
  3. Deposit the images in the places in order — as letters on a wax tablet.
  4. Recall by mental walkthrough — visit each place in turn and demand the deposits from their custodians.
  5. Refresh by repetition — run over all the places again and again to refresh the images.

Note: "memory palace" is a modern phrase; ancient and medieval sources speak of loci (places) or "backgrounds."

The Renaissance Explosion

Ramon Llull (c. 1232–1316) — combinatorial wheels

Llull's Ars magna (c. 1305) used concentric rotating paper disks (volvelles) inscribed with letters B–K standing for divine attributes — goodness, greatness, eternity, power, wisdom, will, virtue, truth, glory. Rotating the disks generated every possible combination of concepts: a quasi-mechanical art of discovery and proof. Yates argues that in the Renaissance this Lullist combinatorics was absorbed into the art-of-memory tradition — "Lullism as an Art of Memory."

Giulio Camillo (1480?–1544) — the Theatre of Memory

A room-sized wooden structure on the Vitruvian theatre plan, but reversed: a single visitor stands on the stage and gazes out at images arranged in seven grades named for the seven planets — Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn — resting on "Solomon's Seven Pillars of Wisdom," ascending from elemental nature (the Banquet, the Cave, the Gorgons) to the arts and sciences. Camillo dictated the explanatory treatise L'idea del theatro from his sickbed about three months before his death; it was published at Florence in 1550. The Theatre was seen by Francis I of France; Erasmus mocked it (letter of 5 July 1532) as liable to excite as great a "tragedy in study" as Luther had in religion.

Yates reads it as the moment the classical art, "transformed by the devout Middle Ages into corporeal similitudes," was "transformed again into magically powerful images." Yates disputed — several historians dissent from the magical reading.

Giordano Bruno (1548–1600) — memory magus

Trained as a Dominican in the art of memory, Bruno published De umbris idearum ("On the Shadows of Ideas") presenting a "Hermetic secret" of memory: concentric wheels — one with 30 segments of astral images combined with wheels of natural objects and of the inventors of the arts — fusing classical loci-and-images with Lullist combinatorics and astral magic. Yates read the wheels as "a means of expressing the total structure of reality by rotating and combining symbols from the elemental, celestial, and intellectual realms." Yates disputed — Sturlese and others argue the wheel-signs are arbitrary, conventional signs: a linguistic generator, not amulets.

Ramism — the counter-tradition

Petrus Ramus's "dialectic method" made logical order itself the memory system and rejected Ad Herennium imagery. The 1584 English controversy between the Brunian Alexander Dicson (defending artificial/Hermetic memory) and the Ramist William Perkins is Yates's chapter XII.

Matteo Ricci (1552–1610) — the memory palace in Ming China

The Jesuit carried the art to China: in 1596 Matteo Ricci taught the Chinese how to build a memory palace. He explained its size should match one's ambition — from "several hundred buildings of all shapes and sizes ('the more there are the better it will be')" down to a temple compound, a reception hall, "or even such a homely object as a wardrobe or a divan." He wrote a treatise on the art of memory in Chinese for the Ming elite, using images built on Chinese characters (e.g., two warriors grappling for the character of war/peace) alongside biblical pictures.

Synthesis: Ancient Techniques, Modern Evidence

The ancients got a great deal right — often for the right structural reasons, discovered two thousand years early:

  • Ordered places → chunking. A fixed loci series converts an unstructured list into an organized spatial schema, binding one item per slot — respecting working-memory limits (Miller/Cowan; Baddeley's visuo-spatial sketchpad).
  • Striking images → deep processing. Imagines agentes anticipate levels of processing (Craik & Lockhart 1972): the more elaborated and distinctive the encoding, the more durable the trace.
  • Image + word → dual coding. Places-as-tablet/images-as-letters, and Aquinas's "corporeal images" for "spiritual impressions," describe exactly what Paivio (1971) demonstrated.
  • Spatial scaffolding → the hippocampus. Quintilian's observation that returning to a place revives what happened there is the folk version of Maguire et al.'s (2003) neural finding.
  • Similarity, contrast, contiguity → associative encoding. Aristotle's three laws run beneath every mnemonic link; modern elaborative-interrogation effects work the same way.
  • Revisiting images → retrieval practice. "Run over rapidly in the mind all the original places" treats recall as the training event — the ancient testing effect (Roediger & Karpicke 2006).
  • Piecemeal learning → cognitive load. "Nothing so bad for the memory as being overburdened" (Quintilian) anticipates Sweller (1988).
  • Earnest attention → encoding gatekeeper. Aquinas's "anxiety preserves the figures of images entire" parallels attention as the gatekeeper (Atkinson–Shiffrin).

Limits. The ancients knew nothing of consolidation, LTP, or sleep-dependent memory; their "refresh the images" was continuous rehearsal, not expanding-interval schedules. And Quintilian's own warning stands: loci fit concrete, ordered material better than connected abstract argument — modern studies echo that gains are material-specific and don't transfer to general reasoning.

10 Evidence-Backed Principles for Learners

Each tied to an ancient source and a modern study:

1. Encode in a fixed spatial order. Build a familiar route, one item per station, rehearse it forwards and backwards.
Ancient: Ad Herennium 3.16.29; Quintilian XI.2 · Modern: Maguire (2003); Dresler (2017) — 26→62 words after 6 weeks of loci training
2. Make every image striking and active. Bizarre, emotional, comic, or grotesque beats bland; give them motion.
Ancient: Ad Herennium 3.22.37 (imagines agentes) · Modern: Craik & Lockhart (1972) — deep processing makes durable traces
3. Pair every verbal fact with a visual image. Two linked traces beat one.
Ancient: places-as-tablet/images-as-letters (Cicero; Ad Herennium); Aquinas's "corporeal images" · Modern: Paivio (1971) dual coding
4. Retrieve, don't reread. Walk the route from memory; blank-page recall; self-test.
Ancient: "run over rapidly in the mind all the original places"; Aquinas: "often reflect... custom is a second nature" · Modern: Roediger & Karpicke (2006) — 61% vs 40% at one week
5. Space the repetitions. Return at expanding intervals rather than drilling flat in one session.
Ancient: Aquinas's "often reflect" · Modern: Cepeda et al. (2006); Ebbinghaus (1885) — each relearning flattens the forgetting curve
6. Link the new to the known. Anchor each item by similarity, contrast, or contiguity.
Ancient: Aristotle, De Memoria; Quintilian's anchor and spear; Aquinas's similitudes · Modern: elaborative interrogation / self-explanation (Dunlosky 2013)
7. Learn piecemeal; respect working-memory limits. Chunk into 3–5 groups; master one before adding the next.
Ancient: Quintilian XI.2.27 — "nothing so bad for the memory as being overburdened" · Modern: Cowan (2001) ~4 chunks; Sweller (1988)
8. Sleep on it. Consolidation is biological, not just psychological.
Ancient: no direct equivalent · Modern: Diekelmann & Born (2010); Scoville & Milner (1957)
9. Mix it up. Interleave problem types rather than blocking.
Ancient: no direct equivalent — the loci route is inherently ordered, which is why this is a useful corrective · Modern: Rohrer & Taylor (2007); Bjork (1994)
10. Distrust fluency. Rereading and highlighting feel effective and are not; ease is the enemy of memory.
Ancient: Aquinas — images must be "unwonted," not commonplace · Modern: Roediger & Karpicke (2006); Dunlosky et al. (2013) — rereading/highlighting rated low utility
