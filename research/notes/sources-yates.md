# Notes — Frances Yates, The Art of Memory (1966) (read 2026-10-02)

## Publication
- Frances A. Yates, The Art of Memory. Copyright page: "© Frances A. Yates 1966." (anyflip.com/xaazp/lezd/basic — unofficial mirror of the book; used only to verify contents).
- Yates was associated with the Warburg Institute; her work on Camillo grew from a booklet Ernst Gombrich (Warburg Institute) handed her; "sixteen years of intensive study" before publication (degruyter.com Matussek, index).

## Central thesis (as evidenced by contents)
- The art of memory is a major thread in Western intellectual history, not a marginal trick: from the classical loci-and-images system, through its medieval ethical transformation (memory as part of the virtue of Prudence), to its Renaissance transformation into Lullist and Hermetic-Cabalist systems (Camillo, Bruno), and finally to its role in the emergence of early modern scientific method (ch. XVII).
- Terminology: "it may be misleading to dismiss it with the label 'mnemotechnics'" and "The word 'mnemotechnics' hardly conveys what the artificial memory of Cicero may have been like" (p. 4; verified via en.wikipedia.org "Art of memory," which quotes p. 4).

## Key claims on figures (from book text read on anyflip)
- Three Latin sources: Ad Herennium ("the rhetoric teacher... addressing his rhetoric students as they congregated around him circa 86-82 B.C."; "The artificial memory is established from places and images (Constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus), the stock definition to be forever repeated down the ages"); Cicero De Oratore; Quintilian Institutio Oratoria XI. Quintilian noted Metrodorus of Scepsis found 360 loci in the 12 zodiac signs; L. A. Post's hypothesis: 36 decans × 10 loci each (book pp. 39, 51-100 range).
- Martianus Capella (De nuptiis): "Simonides, a poet and also a philosopher, is held to have invented the precepts of this art, for when a banqueting-hall suddenly collapsed and the relatives of the victims could not recognise (the bodies), he supplied the order in which they were sitting and their names which he had recorded in memory. He learned from this (experience) that it is order which sustains the precepts of memory." (book, ch. III section.)
- Albertus & Aquinas: see medieval notes — the "beautifully symmetrical" argument.
- Lullism (ch. VIII): Llull's combinatory art fused with the classical memory tradition in the Renaissance.
- Bruno (chs. IX, XI, XIII, XIV): Dominican trained in the art; De umbris idearum presents a "Hermetic secret of memory"; wheels of images combining classical loci with Lullist combinatorics and astral magic; the memory magus who "wires" himself to cosmic forces.
- Ramus (ch. X): Ramist method — logical/dialectical order as the true natural memory, hostile to Ad Herennium imagery; Perkins's Antidicsonus (1584) attacked Dicson's Brunian memory.
- Fludd (chs. XV–XVI): Fludd's memory theatre; Yates's speculative identification of its architecture with the Globe Theatre.
- Ch. XVII: art of memory → scientific method (Leibniz's universal characteristic as heir of Lullist/Brunian combinatorics).

## Scholarly criticisms / corrections (verified)
1. Mary Carruthers, "Inventional Mnemonics..." (Connotations 2.1, 1992; connotations.de, index): Yates's work (like Engel's) gives the impression "that learned mnemonics are a development only of 'the art of memory' described in the Rhetorica ad Herennium, the 'artificial memory' scheme of places and images" and "that interest in mnemonics is to be identified with Neoplatonism." Carruthers: "My own researches in a wide range of medieval literatures from late antiquity through the late 15th century indicates that this is much too narrow a characterization."
2. Carruthers, The Book of Memory (1990; 2nd ed. 2008) — now the standard authority; medieval memory was primarily monastic/meditative and compositional, not only rhetorical; the Ad Herennium scheme is one strand among many (en.wikipedia.org; goodreads review citing Carruthers as replacing Yates as go-to authority — treat as reader opinion, but the scholarly shift is real).
3. Hermetic/magical overstatement: Matussek (degruyter): Yates emphasized Camillo's "rootedness in Hermeticism and magic... to the disapproval of many historians." Sturlese (via Kodera, philarchive.org): Bruno's De umbris wheel-signs "are not really 'icons'... therefore, they are not magical, or amulets: they are arbitrary signs"; De umbris as "generatore linguistico" / manual of mental experimentation — a direct correction of the Yates-Liaño-Vinatea magical reading.
4. The Yates thesis that the memory arts fed the scientific revolution (ch. XVII) is widely regarded as speculative; the Leibniz connection is contested (general scholarly consensus; flagged as Yates's own speculative extension).
5. Dialexis/Dissoi logoi fragment (c. 400 BC) contains a memory section outlining features of the classical art (en.wikipedia.org citing Yates pp. 27–30) — Yates herself noted it (ch. II), so this qualifies her Latin-source emphasis rather than refuting her outright.
