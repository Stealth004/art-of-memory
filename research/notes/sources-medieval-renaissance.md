# Notes — Medieval & Renaissance (read 2026-10-02)

## Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q.49, a.1 (primary)
- URL: https://drbo.org/summa/question/28201.htm (English Dominican translation). Read live (verified live).
- Verified: "On the contrary, Tully (De Invent. Rhet. ii, 53) places memory among the parts of prudence."
- Verified: "Now experience is the result of many memories as stated in Metaph. i, 1, and therefore prudence requires the memory of many things. Hence memory is fittingly accounted a part of prudence."
- Verified: "memory not only arises from nature, but is also aided by art and diligence" (citing "Tully... in his Rhetoric [Ad Herenn. de Arte Rhet. iii, 16, 24]").
- The four rules whereby a man perfects his memory (verified verbatim):
  1. "when a man wishes to remember a thing, he should take some suitable yet somewhat unwonted illustration of it, since the unwonted strikes us more, and so makes a greater and stronger impression on the mind... simple and spiritual impressions easily slip from the mind, unless they be tied as it were to some corporeal image."
  2. "whatever a man wishes to retain in his memory he must carefully consider and set in order, so that he may pass easily from one memory to another."
  3. "we must be anxious and earnest about the things we wish to remember" ("anxiety preserves the figures of images entire" — citing Ad Herennium).
  4. "we should often reflect on the things we wish to remember" ("reflection preserves memories... 'custom is a second nature'").

## Yates on Albertus Magnus & Aquinas (secondary, quoting primaries)
- Yates (1966), via book text on anyflip (read 2026-10-02, index): "The definition by 'Tullius' of the three parts of Prudence is quoted by Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas when discussing the virtues in their Summae. And the fact that 'Tullius' makes memory a part of Prudence was the main factor in their recommendation of the artificial memory. The argument was beautifully symmetrical... Tullius in his First Rhetoric states that memory is a part of Prudence; Tullius in his Second Rhetoric says that there is an artificial memory by which natural memory can be improved. Therefore the practice of the artificial memory is a part of the virtue of Prudence. It is under memory as a part of Prudence that Albertus and Thomas quote and discuss the rules of the artificial memory."
- Yates on Albertus, De bono (ed. cit., p. 240): "'the goodness of memory is in the dry and the cold, wherefore melancholies are called the best for memory.'" Albertus holds artificial memory belongs to reminiscence (recollective search), tied to the "inspired" (dry-hot) melancholy of Pseudo-Aristotelian Problemata (anyflip 51-100).
- Yates on Bartolomeo da San Concordio, Ammaestramenti degli antichi IX.viii (pre-1323): eight rules for memory mainly based on Thomas — order (from Aristotle via Thomas), similitudes/images "not too common ones" (from Summa II-II 49), places and images (from "Tullius in the third book of the New Rhetoric" = Ad Herennium) (anyflip 101-150).
- Yates on English friars (Holcot etc.): used moralized pagan images (fables of the poets) as memory images "as allowed by Albertus Magnus"; "the artificial memory may be a hitherto unsuspected medium through which pagan imagery survived in the Middle Ages" (anyflip 101-150).

## Ramon Llull
- Ars magna (c. 1305): concentric rotating paper disks (volvelles) inscribed with letters B–K representing divine attributes (goodness, greatness, eternity, power, wisdom, will, virtue, truth, glory); rotating wheels generate all possible combinations/propositions — a general art of discovery and proof, aimed at theological demonstration (archania.org; ncregister.com; medienkunstnetz.de). Read via search (index).

## Giulio Camillo (1480?–1544)
- Memory Theatre: room-sized wooden construction in Vitruvian theatre style; spectator stands on the stage looking out at the auditorium, where images are set among seven grades/levels; seven sections named for the seven planets (Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn); resting on "Solomon's Seven Pillars of Wisdom"; upper levels: the Banquet, the Cave, the Gorgons, Pasiphae, the Sandals of Mercury, Prometheus (arts and sciences) (en.wikipedia.org Giulio Camillo; degruyter Matussek; wendtroot).
- Dictated L'idea del theatro from his sickbed ~3 months before death; published Florence 1550 (degruyter; theses.gla.ac.uk).
- Seen by Francis I of France and by Viglius (degruyter). Erasmus mocked it (letter 5 July 1532: it could excite as great a "tragedy in study" as Luther produced in religion) (en.wikipedia.org).
- Yates's reading (via wendtroot, quoting Yates): "The emotionally striking images of classical memory, transformed by the devout Middle Ages into corporeal similitudes, and transformed again into magically powerful images. The religious intensity associated with mediaeval memory has turned in a new and bold direction. The mind and memory of man is now 'divine'..."

## Giordano Bruno
- De umbris idearum (Paris; 1582 per general scholarship — year not verified in this session's sources, use with care): presents memory wheels; one wheel with 30 segments of astral images combined with wheels of natural objects and inventors of arts (goodreads review summarizing Vinatea/Liaño/Yates interpretation).
- Bruno "increases the number of Lullus' wheels by two and employs 750 fields" (Kodera, philarchive.org, index). "Bruno seems to have even claimed he understood the Lullian art better than its inventor did, cf. Yates, Art of Memory, p. 207" (Kodera).
- Yates's Hermetic reading disputed: Rita Sturlese argues the wheel-signs "are not really 'icons'... therefore, they are not magical, or amulets: they are arbitrary signs" and calls De umbris a "generatore linguistico" / manual of "sperimentazione mentale" (Kodera, philarchive.org). Farinella (2002) on Neoplatonism and the wheel (cited in Kodera).
- Yates: Bruno's wheels "not merely [for] mnemonic functions; they were a means of expressing the total structure of reality by rotating and combining symbols from the elemental, celestial, and intellectual realms" (en.wikipedia.org Memory wheels, quoting Yates).
- Ramism vs Brunian memory: 1584 England controversy — Alexander Dicson (De umbra rationis, Brunian, zodiacal/Hermetic imagery) vs William Perkins (Antidicsonus, Ramist: logical/dialectical order as the only legitimate memory) (bookey.app summary of Yates ch. XII, index).

## Matteo Ricci in Ming China
- artofmemory.com/wiki/The_Memory_Palace_of_Matteo_Ricci (index): "In 1596 Matteo Ricci taught the Chinese how to build a memory palace." Size depends on ambition: "several hundred buildings of all shapes and sizes, 'the more there are the better it will be,' said Ricci"; could be modest — temple compound, offices, hostel, merchant's lodge, reception hall, pavilion, studio, or even "the corner of a pavilion, or an altar in a temple, or even such a homely object as a wardrobe or a divan."
- Ricci wrote a book on the art of memory in Chinese for the Ming elite; four ideograph-based images (two warriors grappling, a tribeswoman from the west, a peasant cutting grain, a maidservant) plus religious pictures (bookrags.com summary of Spence, index).
