# Notes — Classical primary sources (read 2026-10-02)

## Cicero, De Oratore 2.86 (Simonides story)
- URL: https://artofmemory.com/blog/simonides-of-ceos/ — reproduces Loeb translation by E. W. Sutton & H. Rackham; page cites www.utexas.edu as source. Read via text fetch (index).
- Verified passage (Sutton/Rackham): Simonides dining with Scopas at Crannon in Thessaly; poem with Castor and Pollux digression; half fee refused; called outside by two young men; roof collapses; Simonides identifies the crushed bodies by remembering each guest's place at table; infers "the best aid to clearness of memory consists in orderly arrangement"; "persons desiring to train this faculty must select localities and form mental images of the facts they wish to remember and store those images in the localities... we shall employ the localities and images respectively as a wax writing tablet and the letters written on it."

## Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria XI.2
- URL: http://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/11B*.html (LacusCurtius; H. E. Butler Loeb translation). Read live (verified live).
- Key verified quotes:
  - §1: "Some regard memory as being no more than one of nature's gifts; and this view is no doubt true to a great extent; but, like everything else, memory may be improved by cultivation." ... "memory has been called the treasure-house of eloquence."
  - §11-16: Simonides story; §14 notes "great disagreement among our authorities as to whether this ode was written in honour of Glaucus of Carystus, Leocrates, Agatharcus or Scopas, and whether the house was at Pharsalus... or at Crannon, as is stated by Apollas Callimachus, who is followed by Cicero, to whom the wide circulation of the story is due."
  - §16: "For my own part, however, I regard the portion of the story which concerns Castor and Pollux as being purely fictitious."
  - §17: "The achievement of Simonides appears to have given rise to the observation that it is an assistance to the memory if localities are sharply impressed upon the mind, a view the truth of which everyone may realise by practical experiment."
  - §18-20: choose a spacious varied place (e.g., house with rooms); symbols (anchor for navigation, weapon for warfare); "The first thought is placed, as it were, in the forecourt; the second, let us say, in the living-room."
  - §21: "We require, therefore, places, real or imaginary, and images or symbols, which we must, of course, invent for ourselves."
  - §22: quotes Cicero verbatim: "We must for this purpose employ a number of remarkable places, clearly envisaged and separated by short intervals: the images which we use must be active, sharply-cut and distinctive, such as may occur to the mind and strike it with rapidity."
  - §23-26: reservations — useful for names in order (auction/Hortensius example); less service for a set speech; "will not the flow of our speech inevitably be impeded by the double task imposed upon our memory?"
  - §27: "If a speech of some length has to be committed to memory, it will be well to learn it piecemeal, since there is nothing so bad for the memory as being overburdened."
  - Also notes Metrodorus of Scepsis found 360 places in the 12 zodiac signs (§22, 26); Charmadas also mentioned.

## Rhetorica ad Herennium, Book 3 (c. 86–82 BC)
- Dating 86–82 BC verified in Yates (book text: "circa 86-82 B.C."), MDPI paper ("ca. 84 BC"), Carruthers via gutenberg-e ("dated 86–82 B.C."). Authorship: anonymous ("pseudo-Cicero"); once attributed to Cicero (booksoftitans; academia.edu).
- 3.16.29 Latin line verified: "Constat igitur artificiosa memoria ex locis et imaginibus" (forum.artofmemory.com). Translation verified (Caplan Loeb via MDPI): artificial memory established from places and images; loci must form a series in fixed order, traversable forwards or backwards from any point.
- 3.22.37 striking images verified (Caplan Loeb via J. Enders, academia.edu): "We ought, then, to set up images of a kind that can adhere longest in the memory. And we shall do so if we establish likenesses as striking as possible; if we set up images that are not many or vague, but doing something; if we assign to them exceptional beauty or singular ugliness; if we dress some of them with crowns or purple cloaks, for example, so that the likeness may be more distinct to us."
- Refreshing images verified (via Santo, rcaap, quoting the treatise): "again and again to run over rapidly in the mind all the original places in order to refresh the images."
- Yates (book) renders the wax-tablet comparison: "For the places are very much like wax tablets or papyrus, the images like the letters, the arrangement and disposition of the images like the script, and the delivery is like the reading."

## Aristotle, De Memoria et Reminiscentia (J. I. Beare trans.)
- URL: http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/memory.html (Internet Classics Archive). Read via search result text (index).
- Verified: "Acts of recollection, as they occur in experience, are due to the fact that one movement has by nature another that succeeds it in regular order."
- Verified: "This explains why we hunt up the series (of kineseis) having started in thought either from a present intuition or some other, and from something either similar, or contrary, to what we seek, or else from that which is contiguous with it."
- Verified: single striking experiences can impress more deeply than repeated ones ("hence upon seeing some things but once we remember them better than others which we may have been frequently").
- Aristotle on place aiding recall, De Mem. ii: "Sometimes a place brings memories back to us: the reason being that we pass quickly from the one to the other" — verified as quoted by Aquinas, Summa II-II q.49 a.1 (drbo.org).
