## A. Sorting and targeting what you study

### A1. PACER — sort information by type, treat each type differently [Sung]
- **Problem it solves:** one technique applied to everything is why study systems break down; flashcards where mind maps belong and vice versa.
- **Steps:** while reading, sort everything into five types and apply the matched treatment —
  - **P — Procedural** (how-to): *Practice* — actually do the thing; it only sticks through immediate practice, not re-read notes.
  - **A — Analogous** (relates to what you already know): *Critique* — how good is the analogy? where does it break?
  - **C — Conceptual** (the what: facts, theories, relationships): *Map* — mind maps, diagrams, network-based notes.
  - **E — Evidence** (makes concepts concrete): *Store and Rehearse* — solve problems, answer questions.
  - **R — Reference** (nitty-gritty specifics): *Store and Rehearse* — flashcards acceptable here.
- Also teaches: balance **consumption** (absorbing) vs **digestion** (processing into long-term memory); overconsumption without digestion → forgetting.
- **Source:** "How to Remember Everything You Read" — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=okHkUIW46ks (2024; ~9.9M views; exact upload date unverified; timestamps: PACER 4:38, Procedural 5:53, Analogous 9:30, Conceptual 15:18, Evidence 19:32, Reference 22:27). Corroborated by [3rd-party] notes: https://blog.jj5.net/blog/2025/03/10/how-to-remember-everything-you-read/ and https://glasp.co/posts/10-best-study-technique-videos.
- **On icanstudy.com?** No dedicated page found.
- [Synthesis]: PACER operationalizes the Four Layers idea into a *reading-time sorting procedure* — it tells you *which* tool to use *while* you read, where Four Layers tells you *what depth* to reach per pass.

### A2. Displacement Strategy + 4-step framework [Sung]
- **Problem it solves:** learners measure "distance" (hours studied, pages covered, notes written, questions completed, the "1,000-hour rule") which predicts nothing; they need "displacement" (where you actually ended up vs where you started).
- **Core reframe:** maximize displacement per unit of distance. Displacement measures are goal-specific: retention, depth of understanding, complexity of problems you can solve — and you must *test* to see them.
- **The 4 steps:**
  1. **Make win criteria explicit** — break the goal into topics × depth levels: what can be merely memorized vs what must be used in complex ways; build in weekly/fortnightly self-tests ("check yourself before you wreck yourself" — read all learning objectives, rubrics, marking criteria before day one).
  2. **Scout challenge types early** — *content* challenges (unfamiliar/hard concepts → more time, more frequent checks, different strategies) and *timing* challenges (fit into your actual schedule proactively, not reactively).
  3. **Plot landmarks** — displacement milestones layered over the official schedule: self-tests at topic transitions; e.g., start assignments weeks early; present a model/framework of what you've learned to a lecturer after two weeks to check understanding.
  4. **Create the strategy** — design so that every hour creates displacement; deliberately drop inherited traditions/assumptions that only add distance.
- **Source:** "How to Study More in 1 Month Than Most People Do in 12 Months" — transcript verified at https://pickscribe.com/v/e5WDzbm7fY0/ (YouTube URL not separately verified; not cited). He says the framework was refined 2011–2022 and the strategy took him to the top of his Master's cohort at <10% of peers' study time [Sung; self-reported, uncontrolled].
- **On icanstudy.com?** Not found as a named page.
- [Synthesis]: the retention-vs-mastery distinction was in the September report; displacement-vs-distance and the 4-step planning procedure are new. This is his *strategic* layer — it sits above the techniques and decides where they get deployed.

### A3. The Outcome Formula — impact first, then quantity [Sung]
- **Problem it solves:** learners try to study long hours by adding time, then adding focus tricks (Pomodoro etc.), then burn out — because they're missing the third factor.
- **Formula:** dream outcome = quantity × quality; quality = focus × **impact**. Impact = how effective your methods actually are at producing the dream outcome.
- **Key mechanism:** low-impact methods don't just waste time — they *drain focus and increase distractibility* (vicious spiral: more hours → worse focus → more hours). High-impact methods *return* focus (positive spiral: effort spent performing them regenerates concentration, so long sessions get easier).
- **Practical actions:** (1) start with impact, not hours — remove low-impact methods, replace with high-impact ones, *then* let the required time fall out of the method; (2) diagnose your current methods by feel — high-impact feels engaging, involves back-and-forth problem-solving thinking, and takes real effort; low-impact feels tedious, drowsy, monotonous, near-effortless (e.g., falling asleep re-reading; the 200th flashcard).
- **Source:** "How to Study 12+ Hours a Day With Focus" — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZfxHVmNSlQ4 (transcript verified via https://pickscribe.com/v/ZfxHVmNSlQ4; upload date unverified).
- **On icanstudy.com?** Not found as a named page.
- [Synthesis]: this is the *sustainability* layer — it explains why his system can run 12–15-hour days without burnout, and it gives a felt-sense diagnostic ("which boxes do I tick when studying?") that a learner can apply immediately.

## B. Thinking at higher order

### B1. Six Levels of Thinking — aim at Level 5 first [Sung]
- **Problem it solves:** students climb Bloom's taxonomy bottom-up (memorize → understand → apply…) and the forgetting curve forces constant relearning of the lower levels.
- **His levels** (Bloom's Revised Taxonomy, his labels): 1. **Remember** — regurgitate (list/define/state). 2. **Understand** — explain. 3. **Apply** — simple one-to-one problem solving. 4. **Analyze** — comparison (compare/contrast). 5. **Evaluate** — prioritize; judgment ("so what? why is this important?"). 6. **Create** — hypothesize novel answers where none exist (least important for most students).
- **The twist:** don't climb bottom-up — **aim at Level 5 first and work down**, because higher-level thinking encodes stronger memory *at all levels simultaneously*.
- **Practical action:** prompt ChatGPT/Gemini to generate Bloom level-4/5 questions for your subject (curricula and exams are designed around Bloom's, so this also reverse-engineers what will be tested).
- **Source:** "6 Levels of Thinking Every Student MUST Master" — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1xqerXscTsE (~2024-06-07 per a secondary citation; vidIQ lists "2 years ago"; ~3.6M views; exact date unverified). Fills the September "video-only" gap.

### B2. GRINDE confirmed as Sung's published Perfect-Mindmap checklist [Sung — verified]
- **What was verified:** the video "The Ultimate Guide to The Perfect Mindmap" (ytscribe titles it "How to Create The Perfect Mindmap (Advanced Guide)") — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Grd7K7bJVWg — teaches **G**rouping, **R**elational, **I**nterconnected, **N**onverbal, **D**irectional, **E**mphasized on camera, verbatim matching the user's framework document.
- **Details from the transcript** (paraphrased): Grouping — the *decision process* of categorizing builds memory. Relational — show how concepts relate (causes, influences, similarities); avoid too few or too many links. Interconnected — zoom out so groups form a coherent whole; anti-pattern: **"Islands"** (dense isolated clusters). G+R+I = a knowledge schema. Nonverbal — cut words; use lines/arrows/spatial layout (generation effect); use abstract **"memory landmarks"** as visual anchors. Directional — arrows showing flow force you to examine each relationship's true nature. Emphasized — judge which relationships matter most (Bloom's level 5) and render the map's **"backbone"**; re-examining importance triggers productive rebuilding (the recursive nature of deep learning).
- **AI guidance [Sung]:** AI helps when it *verifies groups you formed yourself* or saves collecting time; it's harmful when it does the organizing/judging for you.
- [Synthesis]: this is the strongest provenance upgrade in this pass — the user's GRINDE document is confirmed as Sung's public teaching, terms included. The user's document adds worksheet/checklist/spacing scaffolding *around* the six steps; the six steps themselves are his.

## C. Practice drills (concretizing the September definitions)

### C1. Effort/Time Exchange drills [Sung]
- **Problem it solves:** learners try to *reduce effort*; the move is to reduce the effort you *pay for later with relearning time*.
- **Drills:** while reading, constantly **judge importance**; while note-taking, **think about what and how to write** (don't transcribe); **always attempt recall before flipping a flashcard**. Session audit question: *"Am I reaching the right level of struggle?"* — a session counts if you struggled and generated; it doesn't count if you merely transcribed.
- **Source:** "How to Learn ANYTHING Faster Than Everyone" — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qOjSJVaBV94 (transcript via ytscribe; timestamps: Principle 1 at 0:36).

### C2. Omni-Learner drills [Sung]
- **Problem it solves:** fixed "learning styles" (VARK) are a myth **[Established science: Pashler et al. 2008]**, but fluency in *all* modalities is a real edge.
- **Drills:** ask the magic question constantly — **"How can I organize this?"** Treat texts as raw material to interrogate; mentally rearrange diagrams; while listening, offload-then-organize; practice on reading first (easiest modality to start with).
- **Source:** same video as C1 (Principle 2 at 5:55).

### C3. Iteration Effect drills [Sung]
- **Problem it solves:** learners test to *validate confidence*; the move is learning = hypothesis about connections → early frequent testing → refine.
- **Drills:** **micro retrieval** (immediately reconstruct what you just learned, book closed); **scheduled weekly testing aimed at finding mistakes/gaps**; tests that are **deliberately difficult and pitched at real-application complexity**; **apply knowledge immediately after learning**.
- **Source:** same video as C1 (Principle 3 at 16:08).

### C4. Interleaving Rules 1–4 [Sung — partially retrieved, exact wording unverified]
- **Problem it solves:** blocked practice feels fluent but builds fragile knowledge.
- **Rules** (from an AI-generated video summary — exact wording unverified): **Rule 1** — compare and contrast variations of concepts. **Rule 2** — interleave items similar enough to share meaningful similarities but distinct enough to tell apart (his example: mitosis vs meiosis); avoid pairs too similar or too dissimilar. **Rule 3** — force compare-and-contrast frequently *within* sessions rather than on separate days (benefits fade with long gaps). **Rule 4 [uncertain]** — compare every 10–15 minutes using a fresh example, without learning too much first (avoids overwhelm).
- **Corroborated from transcript:** interleaving vs blocked practice (taekwondo paddle story), meta-analysis Hedges g ≈ 0.65 **[Established science]**, the "bathtub vs test tube" analogy (interleaving = slow but robust), and that **high-quality encoding matters as much as** interleaving + retrieval.
- **Source:** "After 10,000 Hours of Studying, I Discovered The Best Learning Technique" — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XeKMjwiF3Pc (transcript cut off before the rules segment; rules via videohighlight AI summary).
- [Synthesis]: Rule 2's similarity "sweet spot" is the most actionable piece — it operationalizes *which* topics to interleave, the question the September report left open.

## D. System-level upgrades (from the free site)

### D1. HUDLE™ — Holistic Upskilling for Deep processing and Learning Efficiency [Sung]
- **Problem it solves:** techniques fail when the enablers are missing; learners collect tips instead of training skills.
- **The four parts:** **Holistic** — techniques need enablers (mindset training, time/task management, focus and distraction management, goal-setting/planning); train *both*. **Upskilling** — learning is a skill trained like an instrument or sport: balance declarative/procedural/conditional knowledge; assess conscious competence of component *and* composite skills; embed experiential reflective practice; build monitoring/self-regulation; give frequent feedback. **Deep processing** — trainable, not fixed; "learning happens in the brain, not in their notes or apps." **Learning efficiency** — evaluate methods on quality of learning + ability to use knowledge + time cost, not retention alone.
- **Training cycle:** consume theory online → practice offline with instructions → get feedback → shorten the loop with self-feedback (metacognition) → social learning.
- **Source:** https://prod.icanstudy.com/hudle (full page read). Also a PDF version exists on icanstudy.com/how-it-works.
- [Synthesis]: HUDLE is the *program architecture* behind the techniques — it explains why his curriculum sequences the way it does (High-Yield Phase → Growth Phase) and legitimizes the "replace, don't add" rule below.

### D2. Free help-center practical rules [Sung]
- **"Replace, don't add"** — when upgrading methods, *replace* existing techniques rather than stacking new ones on top; only *add* a step when you have no equivalent — and most learners lack a **priming/prestudy** step, so that's the commonest legitimate addition. (Source: https://help.icanstudy.com/en/articles/5791458-how-much-time-do-i-need-for-icanstudy — via search snippet; article not fully opened.)
- **Weekly method-training budgets** — high school 3–5h, university 3–5h, professional 2–3h, postgrad 4–6h; split into **theory time vs practice time**; learning-to-learn can't be crammed — "it's like learning an instrument." (Same article.)
- **1–2 highest-impact changes at a time** — don't overhaul everything at once; start from current methods, pick the top 1–2 optimizations, work those before moving on. Two phases: **High-Yield Phase (~1–2 months)** — notetaking, in-class techniques, exam prep, revision, scheduling, time/procrastination/prioritisation; **Growth Phase (~3–8 months)** — retraining natural information-processing habits. (Source: https://help.icanstudy.com/en/articles/5791492-how-is-the-icanstudy-program-structured — via search snippet.)
- **"Don't build your system around an app"** — build a conceptually sound system first, then find apps to fit it; more apps ≠ more productivity. His stack (paraphrased): mindmapping — Squid (Android) / Concepts (iOS) / MS Whiteboard (Windows); scheduling — Google Calendar; email — Spike; tasks — Amazing Marvin (personal) + ClickUp (team); flashcards — Anki or Flashcards Deluxe; PKM — Roam Research (Notion/RemNote/Obsidian acceptable); screen-time — Clearspace. (Source: https://help.icanstudy.com/en/articles/5984812-what-apps-does-justin-use — via search snippet.)
- **"Leaky bucket"** — "lots of retrieval without proper encoding is like filling a leaky bucket faster"; most study methods over-focus on retrieval; better encoding reduces wasted relearning. (Source: https://help.icanstudy.com/en/articles/5788956-how-do-the-techniques-work — opened in full.)
- **Also on the How-it-works page** (https://icanstudy.com/how-it-works): free technical PDFs — the 2022 *Report on Learning* (already known), a **Map of Learning** one-page diagram (labels include multi-relational schematic thinking, prioritisation, expected-value/positional decision making, burnout prevention; visual — text fragmented), and a **Curriculum overview PDF** (contents unverified — download-link resolution failed).
