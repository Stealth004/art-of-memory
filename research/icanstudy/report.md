## 2.1 Bear Hunter System (BHS)

**[Sung]** His flagship named method from the 2022 report — the encoding engine everything else supports. Publicly disclosed sequence:

1. **Identify keywords and terminology** for the topic.
2. **Restricted inquiry → self-explanation → main chunks.** Ask bounded questions that trigger spontaneous self-explanations: evaluate relationships between keywords; ask about their *functional or conceptual importance*.
3. **Restricted inquiry again** to identify relationships *between* concepts/chunks and assign **strict relative priority** to those relationships.
4. **Represent ideas and relationships nonlinearly** (mind-map-like), using illustrations where useful.
5. **Recurse:** repeat restricted inquiry; separate genuinely isolated elements for spaced retrieval or rote memorisation.

Core features: **restricted inquiry** (bounded, not open-ended questioning) to identify commonalities and create chunks; strict prioritisation of relationships; multistage development of domain schemas; nonlinear representation; recursive encoding/retrieval; **reverse thinking** — learn higher-order structure *before* lower-order details; layered progressive complexity; curiosity/relevance guides learning order; rote memory is reserved only for residual isolated details.

- **When to use:** conceptually dense, high-interactivity material (medicine, law, STEM theory) where the exam rewards understanding and transfer.
- **Common mistakes:** rote-memorising details before the schema exists; open-ended "why?" chains that explode cognitive load (restricted inquiry is deliberately bounded); linear note-taking that captures sentences instead of relationships.
- **Caveat [Synthesis]:** the report explicitly says the *full* procedural steps are not publicly disclosed — the complete BHS is taught inside the paid programme. Public material teaches its component principles (below).

## 2.2 Three Cognitive Pillars of Learning

**[Sung]** From https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hz4JKqoG_Mc — "three processes that help you build mastery," applicable "whether you're learning a language, or you're studying medicine, law, or STEM, whether you're in high school or at university, or whether you're learning a new skill at work." He distinguishes **retention** (how long knowledge stays available) from **mastery** (quality, integration, usability of knowledge). The three pillars:

### Pillar 1 — Schema Construction
1. **Skim through all your resources** — don't read deeply yet.
2. **Collect keywords** (big ideas) on one page — this reduces split-attention effect.
3. **Use what you already know** — prior knowledge is raw material.
4. **Take a guess** at the initial relationships between keywords.
5. **Create only a rough draft schema** — deliberately incomplete.
6. Do NOT dive into the detail.

### Pillar 2 — Schema Assimilation
1. Consume new information in **small units**.
2. For each unit, ask: **how does this connect to the schema I already have?**
3. Start with **easy, approachable concepts**; defer dense details.
4. Add knowledge in **layers**.

### Pillar 3 — Schema Reorganisation
1. **Stop consuming** new information.
2. Regroup, simplify, remove, relabel, and redraw the relationships.
3. Do this roughly **every 10–15 minutes** of university-level study.
- **Common mistakes:** assimilating for hours before ever reorganising (then "you're not going to have a good understanding"); polishing a rough draft into a finished product at construction time.

## 2.3 Four Layers of Learning

**[Sung]** From https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2rtSSM-C91I. Study by **repeatedly scanning the material, each time filtering for a different layer** — Layer 1 first, then 2, then 3–4:

1. **Logic Layer** — main ideas, big picture, overall structure, context. Skim, collect keywords, group by similarities/purpose/process/relation. Keep it simple enough to explain to a 10-year-old. ~10–15 minutes for a 1–2-hour topic. *This layer determines how easily the rest learns.*
2. **Concepts Layer** — expand the groups into major concepts with detailed explanation and exam-level conceptual depth. **Spend ~60–70% of total study time here.**
3. **Important Details** — details that clarify or concretise Layer-2 concepts. Add to the map for context; these *may* also go on flashcards. (Flashcards are appropriate here — not at Layers 1–2.)
4. **Arbitrary Details** — residual isolated facts (names, dates) that neither clarify concepts nor fit the big picture. Usually **ignore** unless tested; if required, memorise with flashcards/rote.

- **Common mistakes / red flags:** creating flashcards or trying to hold details in memory very early — a sign you're starting at Layer 3/4.

## 2.4 LADDER Method

**[Sung]** From https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u6ks5OCQR9I — the method he used while working full-time as a doctor (and later to graduate top of his Master's cohort). **Mechanism model:** new information = marbles; the brain's organisational categories = cups. Learning = deciding which cup each marble belongs in based on similarity — that decision *is* the mental effort, and the brain avoids it because organisation is energy-expensive (the brain consumes ~20% of resting energy). With no cups, you face overwhelm (how many cups? which ones? risk of being wrong?).

**Procedure:** break a daunting topic/chapter into successive **low-effort rungs**:
- **Rung 1:** go through the material looking ONLY for what feels easy to understand, easy to compare to what you know, easy to think about organising. Use nonlinear notes to track thoughts. **Skim-read or completely skip** anything that would take too much effort. Result: a working scaffold in ~10–15 min; the topic already feels easier. You can stop here for the day.
- **Rung 2:** same again — look for what now feels easy/lower effort; build on the scaffold, add detail. Takes longer (~35 min in his example) because more now makes sense; the energy shifts from rough understanding to refining decisions and comparisons. Still low-effort.
- **Rung 3:** go through the remaining details — relational detail goes into the map; isolated specifics go to flashcards. Most topics are covered in three rungs.
- **The secret:** each rung should take the *same* effort as the last — because prior rungs reduce later complexity. **Never spend time on hard things** (on a given pass). It gets easier the more you do: stronger cups, structures, foundations. Side benefit: the brain learns studying isn't aversive → less procrastination.
- **When to use:** intimidating topics, dense chapters, starting from zero; also projects/assignments (easy high-level planning first, progressively finer passes).
- **Common mistakes:** forcing hard details too early; treating "finish the chapter" as one monolithic task; believing a pass must cover everything.

## 2.5 Blind Mapping

**[Sung]** Taught as a guided workshop technique (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U0QMMlarSC4, "Private Workshop — How to Learn Anything Faster"): **map before you study.** Select topic keywords *without first mastering them*, then attempt to organise and connect them from memory and prior knowledge — **guessing** at relationships — to build an initial schema; then compare against sources, correct the relationships, reorganise, and repeat. The workshop frames learning as balancing three activities: **consumption, digestion, and testing**. Detailed steps (a "guided walkthrough" occupies the video's 13:38–30:42 segment) are video-only; the public principle is identical to Pillar 1 (Schema Construction) and the "take a guess" step.
- **When to use:** brand-new topics; primes the brain to notice and correct its own errors when it later encounters the material.
- **Common mistakes:** refusing to guess (waiting until you "know enough" to map); treating the first map as a finished product instead of a disposable hypothesis.

## 2.7 Mind Mapping — 3 Levels of Mind Maps

**[Sung]** From https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V8RxHtoLVTk. Mind mapping is a *skill with levels*; level 3 is "legitimately one of the most powerful learning techniques," level 1 "can be a complete waste of time." He claims the level-1 vs level-3 gap roughly separates a top-30% learner from a top-1% learner.

- **Level 0 → 1** (the three processes): (1) use arrows/lines to represent information — **no full sentences**; (2) cut wordiness, summarise more; (3) get comfortable representing information spatially, without words. Level 1 = nonlinear with connections, but messy; "training wheels" — necessary but no real benefit yet.
- **Level 2** — spend much more time *thinking about how to arrange/organise* the information: 20–30+ seconds of thought before writing anything. **Diagnostic:** if you can write constantly, you're not at level 2. This forces **higher-order thinking**, which he calls the key process for understanding/memory/application (citing an OECD statement on higher-order thinking skills). ~10–15% grade bump from 0→2.
- **Level 3** — three characteristics: (1) **structure and flow are much clearer**; (2) **visual emphasis** — thicker arrows/larger groups mark the more important information and relationships; (3) groups are **intuitive and memorable** — *relabelled with your own intuitive names*, not the taught terminology. The level-2→3 process = **quality**: improve the quality of connections/relationships; identify **alternative** ways of structuring and *judge* which is most intuitive/sticky; everything on the page becomes deliberate and intentional.
- **When to use:** encoding new conceptual material; planning essays and literature reviews (he claims 2–3× faster than linear planning, and a map can be re-reviewed in seconds/minutes vs re-reading an essay).
- **Common mistakes:** "I tried mind mapping, it didn't work" after only reaching level 1; decorating branches instead of improving relationship quality; keeping the textbook's group labels instead of relabelling intuitively.
- **[Synthesis]:** Sung notes virtually every top 1–2% learner *thinks* this way mentally even without drawing maps; the map externalises the thinking so you can track and refine it.

## 2.9 Interleaving (his recommended revision technique)

**[Sung]** From https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XeKMjwiF3Pc ("After 10,000 Hours of Studying, I Discovered The Best Learning Technique"). Definition: during revision, **mix different topics/problem types within a session** rather than blocking one topic at a time. ## 2.10 Effort/Time Exchange

**[Sung]** From https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qOjSJVaBV94. **Definition:** deliberately *increase productive cognitive effort early* in order to *reduce total relearning time later*. Procedure: audit whether your studying creates genuine struggle/generation — test yourself before checking, actively organise rather than merely produce notes. A session counts if you struggled and generated; it doesn't count if you merely transcribed.
- **When to use:** deciding between a "fast" shallow pass and a "slow" deep pass — the deep pass wins on total time.
- **Common mistakes:** choosing the method that *feels* fastest per hour; mistaking effort for failure (see misinterpreted-effort hypothesis, §3).

## 2.11 Omni-Learner Principle

**[Sung]** From the same video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qOjSJVaBV94). **Definition:** rejects fixed VARK-style "learning styles." Train yourself to **extract and reorganise knowledge from any modality** — visual, spoken, written, practical experiences. Organisation, not preferred sensory format, is what matters.
- **[Established science]:** consistent with the literature — the "meshing hypothesis" of learning styles has no credible support (Pashler et al., 2008).

## 2.12 Iteration Effect

**[Sung]** From the same video. **Definition:** continuously form and test hypotheses about the material. Procedure: test early and frequently to **expose errors rather than validate confidence** — immediate "micro retrieval," weekly tests, real applications, projects, difficult questions. Deliberately attempt material you might get wrong.
- **Common mistakes:** only testing what you already know (confidence-validating); delaying testing until you feel "ready."

## 2.13 RAIL Framework (skill learning)

**[Sung]** From https://ytscribe.com/v/pHN7BXpdAPQ. Four stages of complex-skill learning, adapted from the four stages of competence with **stage-specific actions**:

1. **Relevance** — *Signs:* lost, unsure what matters, overwhelmed by variables. *Actions:* exploration; challenging assumptions.
2. **Awareness** — *Signs:* trying the skill, making many mistakes. *Actions:* experimentation, reflection, expert feedback.
3. **Iteration** — *Signs:* can perform correctly sometimes but slowly/inconsistently. *Actions:* varied practice and adjustment; **sequence: accuracy and consistency BEFORE speed**.
4. **Lifelong** — *Signs:* skill is low-effort, fast, habitual. *Actions:* refine and use regularly to prevent skill decay.
- **When to use:** procedural/practical skills (clinical skills, programming, lab technique, languages-as-skill).
- **Common mistakes:** chasing speed before accuracy; skipping the exploration phase and copying expert procedure blindly.

## 2.14 Broken Barrel Approach + Five Dimensions of Learning

**[Sung]** From https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ok0yzQDBKNY. He claims this took him from 20-hour study days to 2-hour days at maintained top marks. Two anti-patterns: the **Silver Bullet approach** ("what's the best technique?") and the **Strengths-First approach**. Learning is a wooden barrel: results reach only the level of the **weakest plank**. The five dimensions:

1. **Deep Processing** (the "engine") — extracting meaning, forming strong memories, connecting ideas, solving complex problems. Weakness signs: studying a lot with little to show; slow to understand; can't apply knowledge in new forms.
2. **Self-Regulation** (the "driving") — awareness of how you learn and ability to adjust methods across subjects, difficulty, time pressure, and energy. Weakness signs: don't know where to start; can't predictably change results by changing techniques. Grows in importance in later/competitive years.
3. **Mindset** — response to uncertainty, mistakes, stress, experimentation. Learning-to-learn *requires* trial and error. Weakness signs: avoiding experimentation/mistakes; confidence collapsing after poor results; excessive clarifying questions to reduce uncertainty. He reports **70%+ of assessed learners are limited here** — the biggest transformations he saw were mindset-limited learners.
4. **Retrieval** — how you recall and self-test. Strength: a real spaced schedule, self-testing at multiple difficulty levels. Weakness: no review; challenge level doesn't match real knowledge use. Many students score well here (they know about flashcards) without results.
5. **Self-Management** — time/task priorities, focus, distractions, procrastination; sustainability and consistency.

**Procedure (Broken Barrel):** diagnose your weakest dimension → improve it until it's no longer the weakest → re-evaluate (**every 3–4 months**) → repeat. "Personalised means effective." A free quiz assesses the dimensions (validated in a study with Monash University across ~20,000 learners, per his claims).

# 3. Learning Science Foundations

## 3.1 The memory model he teaches

**[Sung/Established science]:** His report adapts the **Atkinson–Shiffrin multistore model** (1968): sensory input → encoding/processing → storage → retrieval or forgetting; retrieval can facilitate re-encoding; competence at one mastery level does not guarantee competence at another. Forgetting follows **Ebbinghaus (1885)** curves.

## 3.2 Cognitive load theory — his six principles

**[Sung]** His report explicitly teaches six CLT principles (after Sweller and colleagues):
1. **Information Store Principle** — long-term memory stores vast organised knowledge.
2. **Borrowing and Reorganising Principle** — most new knowledge is borrowed from others and reorganised.
3. **Randomness as Genesis Principle** — genuinely novel knowledge comes from random generate-and-test during problem solving.
4. **Narrow Limits of Change Principle** — working memory is severely limited in capacity and duration when handling novel information.
5. **Environmental Organising and Linking Principle** — working-memory limits vanish for organised knowledge retrieved from long-term memory.
6. **Self-Regulation Principle** — learners can regulate their own cognitive processes.

**[Established science]:** These are Sweller's well-known principles of human cognitive architecture (e.g. Sweller, 2010; Sweller, Ayres & Kalyuga, 2011). Sung's application is distinctive: he uses them to argue for *deliberately inducing* germane load through inquiry and organisation (see below), rather than only minimising load.

**Other CLT effects he names:** split-attention, redundancy, variability, expertise reversal, element interactivity, isolated elements, transient information.

## 3.3 Chunking and schemas

**[Established science]:** Working-memory limits are bypassed by **chunking** — Miller (1956), Chase & Simon (1973), and Gobet's template theory. **Schemas** (Bartlett, 1932; Rumelhart; Sweller) are the organised knowledge structures in long-term memory that the Environmental Organising and Linking Principle describes.
**[Sung]:** Chunking is the central mechanism of his whole system — schema construction/assimilation/reorganisation, keyword grouping, LADDER's "cups," and mind-map grouping are all chunking operations. His term **"restricted inquiry"** is his proceduralisation of **self-explanation** (Chi et al.; Renkl's work on worked examples and self-explanations, which he cites) — bounded questioning that forces the learner to generate relationships.
**[Synthesis]:** The mapping is faithful. His distinctive move is making chunk-formation the *explicit, trained skill* rather than a byproduct of study.

## 3.4 Generation, desirable difficulties, and the "misinterpreted effort" hypothesis

**[Established science]:**
- **Generation effect** (Slamecka & Graf, 1978): self-generated material is remembered better than read material.
- **Desirable difficulties** (Bjork, 1994; Bjork & Bjork): conditions that slow initial learning but improve long-term retention/transfer (spacing, interleaving, retrieval practice, varied practice).
- **Levels of processing** (Craik & Lockhart, 1972): deeper semantic processing → stronger memory traces.
- **Elaborative interrogation / self-explanation:** Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate elaborative interrogation and self-explanation as moderate-utility; practice testing and distributed practice as high-utility.
**[Sung]:** His **Effort/Time Exchange**, **Iteration Effect**, and the mind-map level framework are all operationalisations of desirable difficulties. He repeatedly invokes what he calls the **"misinterpreted effort hypothesis"** — learners read effort as evidence of *poor* learning (fluency = learning), when effortful generation actually signals *effective* encoding. **[Established science]:** this maps to the **illusion of fluency** and **cue-utilisation framework** (Koriat, 1997; Koriat & Bjork, 2005 — both cited in his report), and to the broader metacognition literature on miscalibrated judgments of learning (Dunlosky & Metcalfe).

## 3.5 Retrieval practice and spacing — his contrarian stance

**[Established science]:** The **testing effect** (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006) and **distributed practice/spacing** (Cepeda et al., 2006 review) are among the most robust findings in the literature; Dunlosky et al. (2013) give both their highest utility ratings. Practice testing also benefits working-memory-limited learners (Agarwal et al., 2017 — cited in his report).
**[Sung]:** His position, argued at length in the 2022 report, is deliberately contrarian:
- Spaced retrieval is useful but **wildly overgeneralised**; effect sizes are generally moderate and heterogeneous.
- Exact real-world schedules are **not well established** — lab schedules don't transfer cleanly to curricula.
- He hypothesises an **inverted-U**: heavy repetition can hurt efficiency and wellbeing when encoding is weak.
- Therefore: **use spaced retrieval to augment an encoding-optimised system, never as the system itself.** His report cites Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), Latimier et al. (2021), Bahrick et al. (1993), and Bahrick & Hall (2005) in this discussion.
**[Synthesis]:** The *direction* of his critique is defensible — Dunlosky et al. themselves note boundary conditions, and the "desirable difficulties" literature agrees retrieval works best on well-encoded material. But his framing understates the consensus: for durable retention of discrete knowledge, retrieval practice + spacing remain the highest-utility techniques known. His distinctive contribution is the **sequencing claim** (encode first, retrieve second) and the **Layer 3/4 restriction** (flashcards only for details, never for foundations) — both reasonable, though the strong version ("flashcards early = red flag") is his pedagogical rule, not an experimental finding.

## 3.6 Notetaking

**[Sung]:** His report reviews the literature: handwriting vs typing has **no universal winner**; what matters is **cognitive processing before writing**; verbatim notes correlate with weaker delayed retention; structured notes often help, conditional on cognitive load and learner skill. He identifies **five loads in notetaking**: comprehension, identifying key points, linking to prior knowledge/notes, paraphrasing, transforming into documented form. His prescription — delay writing, think first, nonlinear/relational notes — follows directly.
**[Established science]:** Consistent with Jansen et al. (2017) review and Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014, longhand vs laptop — verbatim overlap predicts worse conceptual performance), both cited in his report.

## 3.7 Interleaving, dual coding, multimedia

**[Established science]:**
- **Interleaving** (Taylor & Rohrer, 2010; Rohrer & Taylor, 2007): interleaved practice improves discrimination and transfer in mathematics; benefits are strongest when categories are confusable.
- **Dual coding** (Paivio, 1971, 1986): verbal + visual codes strengthen memory.
- **Multimedia learning** (Mayer, 2001/2009): people learn better from words + pictures than words alone, under coherence/contiguity/modality constraints.
**[Sung]:** Interleaving is his named revision technique (§2.9); his nonlinear maps with illustrations are dual-coding/multimedia in practice (diagrams + minimal words + spatial layout). He does not appear to teach Paivio/Mayer by name in the public material reviewed — the principles are embedded in the method, not the vocabulary.

## 3.8 Inquiry-based learning

**[Established science]:** Bruner (1961) on discovery learning; the modern consensus (e.g. Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006) is that *unguided* discovery fails novices due to working-memory limits, while *guided* inquiry with scaffolding works.
**[Sung]:** His **restricted inquiry** is explicitly designed as the guided middle path — bounded questions that trigger self-explanation without the load explosion of open discovery. **[Synthesis]:** This is a faithful application of the guided-discovery consensus, and arguably his most original pedagogical contribution: a *procedure* for generating germane load on demand.

## 3.9 Metacognition and self-regulated learning

**[Established science]:** Self-regulated learning (Zimmerman; Pintrich) and metacognitive monitoring/control (Dunlosky & Metcalfe; Koriat & Bjork, 2005) predict achievement. The Five Dimensions map closely onto SRL components.
**[Sung]:** The **Five Dimensions** (deep processing, self-regulation, mindset, retrieval, self-management) are his practitioner repackaging of SRL; the **Broken Barrel** is his diagnostic/prioritisation procedure for it; the **14-Step Guide** opens with Measure/Have (goal-setting and planning — classic SRL forethought phase).

## 3.10 Where he diverges from — or goes beyond — consensus

1. **Encoding-first sequencing** (BHS reverse thinking; flashcards only at Layer 3/4): pedagogically motivated, theoretically coherent (schemas reduce intrinsic load for later retrieval), but the *strong* version is his doctrine, not an experimental result.
2. **Inverted-U of repetition:** his hypothesis; not an established finding.
3. **"Take a guess" / blind mapping:** aligns with the **pretesting effect** (Richland et al.; Kornell — attempting answers before study improves later learning even when guesses are wrong), though he doesn't cite that literature in the material reviewed. **[Established science]:** the pretesting effect is real and supports his practice.
4. **Outcome magnitude claims** (20 hrs → 2 hrs; 30% less time; top-1% framing): anecdotal/marketing; the mechanisms are plausible but the magnitudes are not experimentally established.

# 4. Practical Application

## 4.1 End-to-end workflow (first encounter → exam day)

**[Synthesis]** assembled from the 14-Step Guide (§2.18), Three Pillars (§2.2), Four Layers (§2.3), LADDER (§2.4), and BHS (§2.1):

1. **Measure & Have (plan):** define the outcome (what does success look like?); set purpose, plan, schedule.
2. **Prime:** skim all resources; collect keywords on one page (Layer 1 — Logic, ~10–15 min); build a rough-draft schema by *guessing* relationships (Blind Mapping / Schema Construction). LADDER Rung 1: only what's easy.
3. **Assimilate in layers:** consume in small units; connect each unit to the schema; start easy, defer dense detail (Schema Assimilation; LADDER Rungs 2–3; Layer 2 — Concepts, ~60–70% of time).
4. **Reorganise every ~10–15 min:** stop input; regroup, simplify, remove, relabel, redraw (Schema Reorganisation). Restricted inquiry throughout: bounded questions about relationships and relative priority (BHS steps 2–3); represent nonlinearly (BHS step 4).
5. **Details last:** Layer-3 details into the map; Layer-4 arbitrary facts to flashcards/rote (BHS step 5).
6. **Iterate & test:** micro-retrieval during learning; weekly tests; test early to expose errors (Iteration Effect). When confused: explore, turn it into a question.
7. **Revise by interleaving** (§2.9); spaced retrieval as a supplement, revising only after ~20–30% forgetting.
8. **Exam simulation:** test in the same style, level, and cue format; feedback only after genuine effort; replicate intensity, nervousness, and timing.

## 4.2 Subject adaptations

- **Medicine/STEM (high element interactivity):** full BHS; Layers 1–2 dominate; interleaving shines where problem types are confusable (e.g. differential diagnoses, physics problem types). **[Sung]** notes his audience skews medicine/STEM.
- **Law (relational, precedent-based):** schema-first mapping of principles → cases as Layer-3 concretisation; strict prioritisation of relationships mirrors legal reasoning about which precedents control.
- **Languages:** keyword collection + guessing maps onto vocabulary networks; isolated vocabulary = classic Layer-4 flashcard material — the one domain where his system explicitly blesses heavy spaced retrieval (cf. Bahrick et al., 1993, on foreign-language vocabulary, cited in his report).
- **Skills (clinical, programming, lab):** RAIL framework — relevance → awareness → iteration (accuracy before speed) → lifelong maintenance.
- **Assignments/projects:** LADDER applied to planning — easy high-level pass first, progressively finer passes.

## 4.3 Concrete worked examples from his material

- **Marbles and cups** (LADDER): new facts are marbles; your brain's categories are cups; learning is the effortful decision of which cup each marble belongs in — which is why the brain resists it and why pre-building cups (schemas) makes later learning feel easy.
- **Metacognition article demo** (3 Levels of Mind Maps): the same Wikipedia passage noted three ways — Level 0 (linear transcription, "brain almost asleep," nothing retained), Level 1 (nonlinear but still transcribe-and-connect), Level 2 (20–30 s of thought before writing anything; information "sticks" far better). Takeaway heuristic: *if you can write constantly, you're not thinking hard enough to learn.*
- **Wooden barrel** (Broken Barrel): five planks = five dimensions; water (results) reaches only the shortest plank — fix the weakest link first.
- **Sports coaching** (programme design): 2 hrs/week of coached theory + daily practice in real studying = the iCanStudy dosage model.

## 4.4 Exam-day specifics

**[Sung]** From the 14-Step Guide's Test phase: match the exam's style, level, and cues; withhold feedback until after genuine effort; schedule revision at ~20–30% forgetting; rehearse under matched intensity, nervousness, and time pressure. From the iteration effect: test to expose errors, never to validate confidence.
